The product

Three journeys, one ledger

Companies and investors sign in separately. The raise funds the company, and dividends pay investors back from real profits.

Company

  1. Sign inSEP-10 / passkey
  2. Prove ownershipregistry + domain
  3. Sign X% profit agreementhash on-chain
  4. Set raise termsprice, min, max, deadline
  5. Receive USDCat successful close

Investor

  1. Sign inpasskey wallet
  2. Prove non-US residenceZK proof
  3. Trustline approvedset_authorized
  4. Browse companiessocials, financials
  5. Invest at fixed priceUSDC into escrow
  6. Trade after closeorder book / pool

Dividend loop

  1. Holder locks tokensfull epoch
  2. Company deposits profitsfiat → USDC
  3. distribute()pro-rata to lockers
  4. Holder claims USDCor cashes out

The model

A fixed-price raise, the way private equity works

Private companies raise money in a primary sale: they issue new shares at a fixed price, set from a negotiated valuation, and the cash goes to the company. Later, investors can sell to each other in a secondary sale, and that cash goes to the seller, not the company. This platform does both on-chain.

The raise: all-or-nothing escrow

During the raise

  1. Company sets price, min, max, deadline
  2. Investors deposit USDCSoroban escrow

Minimum reached by deadline

  1. USDC released to companyin full; flat listing fee paid at open
  2. Tokens minted to investorsexactly the amount sold
  3. Mint power removedsupply fixed forever

Minimum missed

  1. Every investor refundedautomatically, in full
  2. No tokens exist
Into the companyThe raise, in USDC

Upfront growth capital. No loan, no collateral, no loss of voting control.

Between investorsSecondary trading

Order book and an optional seeded pool. The company isn't involved.

Out of the companyX% of profits, in USDC

Paid each epoch to holders locked for the whole period.

Pricing from yield: a worked example

The company commits X% of profits, so the raise is priced as that profit stream divided by the yield investors want. Take a business earning $200k a year that commits 50%, which is $100k a year to holders.

Investor target yieldCompany raisesPaid to holders each yearImplied cost of capital
22%$455k$100k22%
18% (base case)$556k$100k18%

The platform charges a flat listing fee per raise, never a share of what is raised, so it isn't paid like a broker (Securities Act §4(b)). In the base case the company keeps the full $556k raised.

Why a company signs up

Cash upfront that never has to be repaid. It costs a share of profits that only exist if the business actually earns them. For comparison, bank loans run about 8–12% and need collateral, and revenue-based financing works out to about 25–38% effective.

There is no buyback. Investors exit by selling to other investors on the secondary market. If the business is sold, the agreement passes the dividend obligation to the buyer.

This is now clearly a securities offering

Selling profit rights for cash is a primary offering. Reg S already handles excluding US persons. In the EU, crowdfunding rules cap raises at about €5M per year (not verified in this research). For a testnet hackathon that belongs on a slide, not on the blocker list. This is not legal advice.

Stellar Unlocked

Five core primitives do the heavy lifting

Most of the design runs on features built into the protocol, not on custom contracts. The ZK functions are new. The asset flags and the order book date from 2015. Combining them is what makes the project fit Stellar Unlocked.

  • BN254 + Poseidon host functions (CAP-74/75, Protocol 25, live since 22 Jan 2026; CAP-80 added MSM and field arithmetic in Protocol 26). Used to verify the investor's residence proof on-chain.
  • Classic asset flags AUTH_REQUIRED | AUTH_REVOCABLE | AUTH_CLAWBACK_ENABLED. A protocol-level allowlist on every venue.
  • Stellar Asset Contract (SAC). Lets the escrow mint the exact amount sold and lets the gate call set_authorized. Since Protocol 26 it can also create trustlines (CAP-73).
  • Native order book (SDEX) + path payments. Secondary trading with no pool required: investors pay in XLM or any asset and receive the share token, among verified holders only.
  • Clawback and claimable balances (CAP-35, CAP-23). Recovery for court orders and lost keys, plus an alternative dividend payout path.

Compliance

Prove residence, and let the protocol enforce it

Spec change: residence, not nationality

Under Regulation S, a US person is anyone resident in the US, plus US-organized entities (17 CFR 230.902). A US citizen living in Lisbon is generally not a US person. A French citizen living in New York is.

zkPassport and Self prove passport attributes such as nationality, and neither has a Stellar integration. So we build our own small circuit:

  • A KYC provider's result becomes a credential signed by the platform (EdDSA over Poseidon).
  • The circuit proves residence_country ≠ US, that the credential hasn't expired, and that it came from a trusted issuer. For companies buying, it proves incorporation outside the US.
  • Public outputs are a Poseidon nullifier and the investor's Stellar address, so a proof can't be replayed or front-run.
  • The gate contract checks the BN254 pairing, then calls set_authorized. From then on the protocol enforces it everywhere.
  • Reg S also requires the buyer to be outside the US when ordering, so add a per-session location check or self-attestation in the UI.

Cost isn't a concern. Nethermind measured an optimized Noir UltraHonk verification at about 80M of the 400M per-transaction instruction limit, costing 0.0124 XLM on testnet. Groth16 should be cheaper; the only figure found (~12M instructions) is unverified. SDF's groth16_verifier example is the starting point. Test snarkjs proof formatting on day one, because its BN254 path was only tested with Gnark fixtures.

Gating and secondary trading

Tokens with transfer hooks need every pool contract registered as an identity. Stellar's classic authorization flags avoid that: order-book trades, path payments and pool shares all fail without approved trustlines. The secondary market is open, but only to verified members.

Legal strategy · draft, not legal advice

Software, not a broker

Decentralizing doesn't remove liability: regulators look at what the team does (Ooki DAO, Tornado Cash). Matching companies with investors, curating raises and taking a cut of each raise is what makes a platform a broker. So dvdnd keeps the code open and drops the role. Each company, or the licensed intermediary it hires, gates its own raise, and the contracts enforce that raise's rules.

Staying outside the broker role (Securities Act §4(b))

  • No pay tied to sales. A flat listing fee per raise, never a share of what is raised. Built.
  • Never holds investor money. Escrowed USDC can only go to the company on success or back to investors. Built; untested for smart contracts, so counsel must confirm.
  • No bad actors among the people behind the platform or a raise. A process, not code.

What the contracts enforce

  • Eligibility per exemption: the ZK residence proof for Reg S; for 506(c), a signed accreditation statement plus a verifier's attestation or at least 200,000 USDC.
  • Lock-up: Reg D raises need at least a year after the deadline (Rule 144). Claimed tokens earn in the dividend vault while the wallet is frozen; afterwards they trade only between eligible holders.
  • A holder cap under the Exchange Act §12(g) trigger (2,000 holders, with $10M+ in assets).
  • Freeze and clawback belong to each raise's compliance account, with a recorded reason, never to dvdnd.

Path

  1. Hackathon: testnet only, no real money.
  2. Protocol + 506(c): companies raise under 506(c) (and Reg S), use an accreditation service and file Form D. dvdnd is software with no transaction fee.
  3. Retail through a partner: Reg CF raises through a registered funding portal using dvdnd's contracts.
  4. Own licences (funding portal, then possibly an ATS for secondary trading) once revenue justifies them.

Architecture

Four contracts, no bonding curve

Registry Soroban

Company profile, KYB attestation hash, agreement hash, payout %, schedule, raise terms, links.

Eligibility gate Soroban · SAC admin after close

BN254 Groth16 verifier, Poseidon credential root, nullifier set. Approves trustlines. Cannot mint.

Raise escrow Soroban

Fixed price, min/max, deadline. Holds USDC, mints exactly what sold at close, pays the company, or refunds everyone.

Lock vault Soroban · custom

Epoch-keyed positions, reward-per-share accumulator, full-epoch eligibility only.

Liquidity seed optional

5–10% of the raise plus matching tokens into a pool, with the pool position locked. Gives passkey users instant trading.

Front end Next.js

smart-account-kit passkeys, OZ Channels gas sponsorship, Freighter via Wallets Kit, an indexer such as Mercury, and the simulation page.

The vault rule that makes the model work

Only tokens locked for the whole epoch earn. Otherwise traders lock right before distribute() and sell right after. Key positions by holder and epoch, extend storage TTLs on every interaction, and never keep user data in temporary storage.

Check this on testnet first: admin hand-off

The plan: the escrow is SAC admin during the raise, mints once at close, then calls set_admin to hand control to the gate. The gate can approve holders but never mint, so supply is fixed by code without locking the issuer key. Confirm SAC admin calls work from a non-issuer contract admin before writing the architecture doc.

Why fixed price

Everyone pays the same price, so everyone earns the same yield

pump.fun's price formula is P(x) = (30 + x)² / (30 · 1.073×10⁹), so the last buyer before graduation pays (115/30)² ≈ 14.7× what the first buyer paid. For a memecoin, that steepness is the product. For a dividend token, it means the first buyers get a yield the business can't support, paid for by everyone after them. A fixed-price raise removes that early-buyer advantage completely.

Sale modelFirst buyer's yieldLast buyer's yieldWho wins
pump.fun-style curve≈ 265%18%Snipers and bots
Fixed-price raise18%18%Everyone the same; the company gets the full raise

Assumes an 18% target yield (a business valued at ~4× distributable profit). On the secondary market, price can move freely, and with it the yield a new buyer gets. That's normal price discovery among holders, and the company has already been funded.

The simulation · its own page in the app

Same traders, two kinds of market

Run the same population of traders against a pump.fun-style memecoin market and against fixed-price dividend raises. The only thing that changes is the asset. Then compare who makes money, and whether real businesses get funded.

Agents

  • Snipers buy in the first blocks and dump. With a fixed price they have no edge.
  • Momentum traders follow price and volume, and turn over quickly.
  • Yield investors invest when the target yield beats a hurdle (8% plus a risk premium), then lock and hold.
  • Secondary buyers and sellers trade on the order book, at a discount or premium to the raise price depending on dividends paid.
  • Companies are honest, pay late, misreport, or fail.

  • LabelSolidus Labs' 98.6% "rug or pump-and-dump" is a vendor classification, not a measured loss rate

Path to production

The code can be ready for regulation; the regulated parts themselves come from vendors, auditors and lawyers. This is the order to do them in. Not legal advice.

1. Legal structure

  • What the token is: a security, a right to a share of profit. Each company needs a legal wrapper (an SPV or a profit-participation agreement) and a transfer agent or registrar keeping the official holder record.
  • Offering rules: Regulation S to exclude US persons; in the EU, a crowdfunding service provider licence (raises capped around €5M a year, to verify) or a prospectus per offering.
  • Models on Stellar: RedSwan (FINRA-regulated digital securities marketplace) and Spiko (AMF/ACPR-regulated tokenized funds) already run regulated securities on the network.

2. Vendors

  • KYC and AML: Sumsub, Persona or Didit for residence and identity, plus sanctions and PEP screening. The vendor's result signs the credential that goes into the ZK tree.
  • Money: Circle Mint for companies; FinClusive (SEP-6, 24, 31), MoneyGram or regional anchors for investors.
  • Security: an audit of the contracts and the ZK circuit (Veridise covers both) and a multi-party trusted-setup ceremony.

3. Code that makes it auditable

  • Admin powers split into roles, with compliance actions behind a multisig and admin changes behind a timelock.
  • A compliance console for freezing, unfreezing and clawback with a stated reason, and an audit log from on-chain events.
  • stellar.toml (SEP-1) describing the organization, its assets and their regulation.
  • Sanctions screening, signed credentials and a verifiable agreement document.
  • Standards to consider: SEP-8 regulated assets for per-transfer approval, and SEP-61 Trustline Onboarder (Authline implements it) for one-step trustline approval from wallets and brokers.

Decisions

  1. How the company gets funded

    Fixed-price raise into an all-or-nothing escrow. Then secondary trading on the order book, with an optional seeded pool. No bonding curve.

  2. Investor eligibility

    A real ZK residence proof: Circom Groth16, verified on BN254 in Soroban.

  3. Simulation

    Its own interactive page inside the web app.

  4. Buyback clause

    None. Investors exit on the secondary market; the obligation follows the business if it is sold.

  5. Platform fee

    A flat listing fee per raise, paid when it opens (replaces the earlier 5% of successful raises). Failed raises are refunded in full. Pool seeding stays optional and off by default.

  6. Compliance model

    dvdnd is open compliance software, not a broker. Each raise picks Reg S or Reg D 506(c), with a lock-up, a holder cap and its own compliance account (the company or its transfer agent).
